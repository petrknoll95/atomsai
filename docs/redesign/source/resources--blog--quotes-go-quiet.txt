Many quotes don’t die of price. They die of silence. A quote goes out by email, the customer has one question about it, and the question arrives somewhere nobody connects to the quote: a text to the technician’s phone, a website chat at 9pm, a reply to an email address nobody checks. Meanwhile the office sends one “just checking in” email and moves on. The fix is an agent that follows up on every open quote across text, email, chat and phone, answers questions from the quote itself, books the next step, and stops the moment the customer replies or says no.

Nobody in either story was careless, and the price wasn’t the problem. Both customers asked a question, and a question about a quote is a buyer telling you what stands between them and yes. The homeowner asked about a thermostat on a Saturday night through the website chat, which knew nothing about his quote. The couple asked about fabric on a Tuesday evening by replying to an email that only one person could see. In both cases the answer came a day and a half later, after someone else had answered first.
Why do quotes go quiet?
1. Follow-up depends on someone remembering.
The quote goes out automatically. The follow-up doesn’t. It sits on a list in the office manager’s head, competing with today’s phone calls, so most quotes get one email or one call, and then nothing. The cost is every quote that would have closed with one more, better-timed message. You notice this when you can’t say, for last month’s quotes, how many were followed up at all.
2. The customer’s question lands somewhere that can’t see the quote.
People answer on whatever channel is in front of them. A text to the technician’s phone, a website chat, a reply to the quote email, a voicemail to the main line. Each lands in a different place, and none of them knows which quote it’s about. So the question waits, or gets a general answer, while the customer is still deciding. You notice this when quotes are marked “lost” and nobody can find the last thing the customer asked.
3. “Just checking in” isn’t follow-up.
A generic nudge asks the customer to do the work: dig out the PDF, remember what confused them, and write back. The messages that move a decision answer the thing they were stuck on, such as what each option includes, what the timeline is, or whether financing is available. And the follow-up has to stop when they say no. A business that keeps texting after “we went with someone else” doesn’t get the next job either. You notice this when your “lost” reasons say “price” but nobody ever discussed price.

What you control.
Which quotes the agent follows up, on which channels, how often and how long. What it can answer on its own: what’s in the quote, timelines, next steps. What it never does: change a price, offer a discount, promise a date that isn’t in your calendar, or keep messaging someone who said no. Texts and calls follow consent and quiet hours. Anything outside its rules goes to a person, with a summary, the quote and the full conversation, and your team can join any conversation live.

Questions buyers ask
How many times should you follow up on a quote?
Enough to answer the questions people actually have, and no more. A common pattern is a text the next day, an email that lays out the options a few days later, and a short call in the second week. The sequence should stop the moment the customer replies, books, or says no.
Can an AI agent answer questions about a quote?
Yes, from the quote itself and the rules you give it. It can explain what each option includes, what changes between them, and what happens next. It never changes a price or invents a discount. Prices and offers come only from your quote and your rules, and it hands anything else to a person with the full conversation.
What if the customer replies on a different channel from the quote?
The agent answers web chat, phone, SMS and email with one memory, so it matches the reply to the open quote by phone number or email. The customer doesn’t have to repeat anything, and the follow-up sequence stops.
Will it keep messaging people who said no?
No. A no, a “not now” or an opt-out stops the sequence at once. The agent records the reason in your CRM, and if someone says “ask me in spring”, it can set a reminder on the date your rules allow.

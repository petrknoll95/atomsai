AI pilots usually stall before production because of what sits between the model’s output and a finished piece of work: writing back to your systems, approvals, the exceptions nobody scoped, and no agreed number for whether it’s working. Fix those, and the model is rarely the problem.
Stalled AI programmes tend to share a shape. A pilot that worked. A steering committee that approved. And, months later, nothing in production.
The post-mortem almost always blames the model, or “change management.” It’s usually neither. It’s the gap between producing an output and finishing a piece of work.
Where does the work actually end?
A demo ends when the model returns text. The work ends when the invoice is paid, the ticket is closed, the policy is bound. Between those two points sit the write-back to the system of record, the approval nobody had authority to design, the exception path for the documents that arrive malformed, the audit trail your controller needs at month-end, and someone on call when a downstream API returns 500 for an hour.
None of that is glamorous and all of it is the project.
Integration is the product.
How do you know if it’s working?
The second reason is measurement. A pilot is judged by whether a room was impressed. Production is judged by whether it’s still trusted in month four — and if you can’t answer “is it working?” with a number, trust decays to zero on the first visible mistake. That’s why we write failure tests before the happy path (
more on evals here
): accuracy against a labelled set, cost per run against a ceiling, latency against a budget, and an alarm when any of them moves.
Which workflow should you automate first?
RFPs name the workflow that’s easiest to describe. The expensive one is the one people are slightly embarrassed about — the shared mailbox, the Friday spreadsheet, the queue with no owner. Look in the exception queue, not the process diagram.
If you want a cheap test of whether a pilot will survive: ask who owns the runbook, what the rollback is, and what number gets checked on Monday. If nobody can answer, the model quality is irrelevant.
Somewhere in your finance team, someone spends Friday fixing the invoices that failed OCR.

Questions buyers ask
Why do AI pilots fail to reach production?
Rarely because of the model. They stall on the work between an output and a finished task: writing back to your systems, approvals, the exceptions nobody scoped, and no agreed number for whether it’s working.
How do you move an AI pilot into production?
Scope the full workflow to the point where the work is finished, including write-backs, approvals and the exception path. Agree the numbers that define success before you build, and name who owns the runbook and the rollback.
Which workflow should we automate with AI first?
Look in the exception queue, not the process diagram: the shared mailbox, the Friday spreadsheet, the queue with no owner. That is usually where the cost is.
How do you know if an AI system is working in production?
Measure it against a labelled set for accuracy, a ceiling for cost per run and a budget for speed, with an alarm when any of them moves.
A demo proves a model can do the task once. Production proves your business can rely on it every time — and almost nothing you built for the first gets you the second.
